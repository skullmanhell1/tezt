Redox Fundamentals

1Definitions of Redox

A redox reaction is one in which electrons are transferred. Oxidation and reduction always occur together — the electrons lost by one species are gained by another.

Redox as the transfer of electrons

Electron definition (the modern, general one)
  • Oxidation = loss of electrons.
  • Reduction = gain of electrons.
OIL RIG

Oxidation Is Loss · Reduction Is Gain (of electrons)

Redox as the transfer of oxygen

Oxygen definition (historical)
  • Oxidation = gain of oxygen.  e.g. 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO (Mg is oxidised).
  • Reduction = loss of oxygen.  e.g. CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O (CuO is reduced).

Redox as the transfer of hydrogen

Hydrogen definition (historical)
  • Oxidation = loss of hydrogen.  e.g. H2S → S + 2H+ + 2e− (S is oxidised).
  • Reduction = gain of hydrogen.  e.g. Cl2 + H2 → 2HCl (Cl is reduced).

2Oxidation Numbers

The oxidation number is the charge an atom would have if every bond were completely ionic. It is the tool used to track electron transfer.

Rules for assigning oxidation numbers
  • Elements are always 0 (e.g. Na, O2, Cl2, S8).
  • Monatomic ions are always equal to the charge of the ion (Na+ = +1, S2− = −2, Al3+ = +3).
  • Hydrogen is always +1 …
  • … except in metal hydrides where it is −1 (e.g. NaH, CaH2).
  • Oxygen is always −2 …
  • … except in peroxides where it is −1 (e.g. H2O2), and in OF2 where it is +2.
  • In a polyatomic ion, the sum of all oxidation numbers must equal the charge on the ion.
  • In a compound, the sum of all oxidation numbers must equal zero.
Worked examples
SO42−: x + 4(−2) = −2 ⟹ S = +6
MnO4−: x + 4(−2) = −1 ⟹ Mn = +7
Cr2O72−: 2x + 7(−2) = −2 ⟹ Cr = +6
NH4+: x + 4(+1) = +1 ⟹ N = −3

3Using Oxidation Numbers

Identifying a redox reaction

How oxidation numbers show a reaction is redox If the oxidation number of any element changes between reactants and products, the reaction is redox. An increase = oxidation; a decrease = reduction. If no oxidation number changes, it is not a redox reaction.

Balancing a redox half-equation — acidic conditions only

The four steps (acidic solution)
  • Step 1: Balance all atoms except O and H (the atom being oxidised/reduced first).
  • Step 2: Balance oxygen by adding H2O.
  • Step 3: Balance hydrogen by adding H+.
  • Step 4: Balance charge by adding electrons (e−); then check atoms and charge both balance.
Example — permanganate reduced in acid
MnO4− + 8H+ + 5e− → Mn2+ + 4H2O
Check: atoms balance (1 Mn, 4 O, 8 H each side); charge = (−1 + 8 − 5) = +2 on the left = +2 on the right ✓

4Writing Net Redox Reactions from Two Half-Equations

What half-equations show

A half-equation shows one process on its own — either an oxidation (electrons on the right, lost) or a reduction (electrons on the left, gained) — together with the electrons involved.

Using the Standard Reduction Potentials table

Reading the data booklet table to get common half-equations
  • Read left to right (as printed) → a reduction half-equation (the species gains electrons).
  • Read right to left (reversed) → an oxidation half-equation (the species loses electrons); reverse the sign meaning of E° when it acts this way.

Combining two half-equations into a net redox equation

The four steps
  • Step 1: A reduction and an oxidation half-equation must both be present (reverse one so one is written each way).
  • Step 2: Multiply each half-equation so the number of electrons is equal in both.
  • Step 3: Add the two half-equations together.
  • Step 4: Cancel the electrons and any species identical on both sides (e.g. H+, H2O); check atoms and charge.
Example — MnO4− oxidising Fe2+ in acid
Reduction (×1): MnO4− + 8H+ + 5e− → Mn2+ + 4H2O
Oxidation (×5): 5Fe2+ → 5Fe3+ + 5e−
Net: MnO4− + 8H+ + 5Fe2+ → Mn2+ + 5Fe3+ + 4H2O
Charge check: left +17 = right +17 ✓

5Terminology: Oxidants & Reductants

Oxidant / oxidising agent

Definition An oxidising agent (oxidant) causes another species to be oxidised by accepting/gaining electrons; the oxidant is itself reduced.

Common oxidising agents: acidified permanganate (MnO4−, purple → colourless Mn2+), acidified dichromate (Cr2O72−, orange → green Cr3+), the halogens (F2, Cl2, Br2), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), oxygen (O2), concentrated nitric acid (HNO3) and Fe3+.

Which acid is used to acidify the oxidising agent — and why? Dilute sulfuric acid (H2SO4). It supplies the H+ ions needed, but its ions are not easily oxidised or reduced, so it does not interfere with the reaction.
  • Not HCl — the chloride ion Cl− can itself be oxidised (e.g. by MnO4− or Cr2O72−) to Cl2, interfering with the result.
  • Not (dilute) HNO3 — the nitrate ion is itself an oxidising agent and would compete with the oxidant being studied.

Reductant / reducing agent

Definition A reducing agent (reductant) causes another species to be reduced by donating/losing electrons; the reductant is itself oxidised.

Common reducing agents: reactive metals (Na, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe), hydrogen (H2), carbon (C), Fe2+, iodide (I−), sulfite/SO2 and oxalate (C2O42−).

The classic trap The oxidising agent is the species that gets reduced; the reducing agent is the species that gets oxidised.

6Standard Reduction Potentials (SRP)

What are they?

The standard reduction potential (E°) is a measure of the tendency of a species to be reduced (to gain electrons), expressed as a voltage measured against a reference half-cell.

Conditions they are measured at

Standard conditions (the "°") Solution concentrations 1.0 mol L−1 · gas pressure 100 kPa · temperature 25 °C (298 K).

What they are measured against (the reference cell)

All potentials are measured relative to the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE), which is defined as exactly 0.00 V.

How they are read from the table

Reading the series
  • All entries are written as reductions, ordered by E°.
  • More positive E° (top) → stronger oxidising agent (more readily reduced), e.g. F2, MnO4−.
  • More negative E° (bottom) → its reduced form is a stronger reducing agent, e.g. Li, K, Na.

How they predict whether a reaction is spontaneous

The test
E°cell = E°cathode(reduction) − E°anode(oxidation)
A positive E°cell ⟹ the reaction is spontaneous. Equivalently (the "anticlockwise rule"): the oxidant higher in the table will oxidise a reductant lower in the table.

7Galvanic (Electrochemical) Cells

A galvanic cell uses a spontaneous redox reaction to convert chemical energy into electrical energy. The two half-reactions are separated so the electrons must travel through an external wire — this is the current.

Cell notation

Anode written on the left. | = phase boundary; ‖ = salt bridge.

anode | anode solution ‖ cathode solution | cathode
e.g.   Zn(s) | Zn2+(aq) ‖ Cu2+(aq) | Cu(s)

Chemical principles of a galvanic cell

The rules (completed)
  • Oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously but in separate locations.
  • Oxidation occurs at the anode (anode & oxidation both begin with vowels).
  • Reduction occurs at the cathode (cathode & reduction both begin with consonants).
  • Electrons flow in the external circuit from anode to cathode (alphabetical order).
  • The salt bridge maintains electrical neutrality (and completes the circuit; it does not carry electrons).
  • Anions flow towards the anode.
  • Cations flow towards the cathode.
Polarity In a galvanic cell the anode is negative (−) and the cathode is positive (+).

Calculating the theoretical cell voltage

Formula
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
Both values taken as reductions from the SRP table. e.g. Zn|Cu cell: (+0.34) − (−0.76) = +1.10 V. E° is intensive — do not multiply it when balancing electrons.

Why the actual voltage differs from the theoretical value

Causes
  • Non-standard conditions — concentrations ≠ 1.0 mol L−1, temperature ≠ 25 °C, or gas pressure ≠ 100 kPa.
  • Internal resistance of the cell and the resistance of the salt bridge cause a voltage drop when current flows.
  • As the cell operates, reactant concentrations fall and product concentrations rise, so the voltage decreases over time.

Identifying reactions & predicting observations

How to identify each electrode reaction and the observations
  • Anodic (oxidation) reaction: the half-cell with the more negative E° (lower on the series); write it reversed as an oxidation.
  • Cathodic (reduction) reaction: the half-cell with the more positive E° (higher on the series).
  • Observations are predicted from the half-equations: the anode electrode dissolves / loses mass; the cathode gains mass (metal deposits) or a gas bubbles off; solution colours change (e.g. blue Cu2+ fades, purple MnO4− decolourises).

8Commercial Galvanic Cells

Three types
  • Primary cell — non-rechargeable; the reaction is not reversible, so it is discarded when the reactants are used up (e.g. dry cell).
  • Secondary cell — rechargeable; the reaction can be reversed by applying an external voltage (e.g. lead accumulator, lithium-ion).
  • Fuel cell — the reactants (fuel + oxidant) are supplied continuously from outside the cell, so it produces electricity for as long as fuel is fed in.

Dry cell (zinc–carbon, Leclanché) — primary

Anodic: Zn(s) → Zn2+(aq) + 2e−
Cathodic: 2MnO2(s) + 2NH4+(aq) + 2e− → Mn2O3(s) + 2NH3(aq) + H2O(l)
AdvantagesDisadvantages
Cheap, portable, made in many sizes; sealed with no free-flowing liquid.Not rechargeable; voltage falls steadily during use; short shelf life (the zinc casing corrodes and the cell can leak).

Lead accumulator (lead–acid) — secondary

Anodic: Pb(s) + SO42−(aq) → PbSO4(s) + 2e−
Cathodic: PbO2(s) + 4H+(aq) + SO42−(aq) + 2e− → PbSO4(s) + 2H2O(l)
AdvantagesDisadvantages
Rechargeable; delivers a large current/surge (good for starting car engines); reliable and relatively cheap; works over a wide temperature range.Very heavy (low energy-to-mass ratio); contains toxic lead and corrosive sulfuric acid (handling/environmental hazard); damaged by repeated deep discharge.

Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell

Acidic electrolyte
Anodic: 2H2(g) → 4H+ + 4e−    Cathodic: O2(g) + 4H+ + 4e− → 2H2O(l)
Overall: 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O
AdvantagesDisadvantages
High efficiency (chemical energy → electricity directly, not via heat); runs continuously while fuel is supplied; only product is water (clean); quiet, no moving parts.Expensive (platinum catalysts and materials); H2 is hard and hazardous to store/transport (flammable, low density); H2 is often produced from fossil fuels, so it is not always "clean".

9Corrosion & Its Prevention

Corrosion is the unwanted oxidation of a metal by its environment. Wet corrosion (rusting of iron) is electrochemical and requires both water and oxygen; it is faster with dissolved salt or acid.

The two half-reactions of rusting
Anodic: Fe(s) → Fe2+(aq) + 2e−
Cathodic: O2(g) + 2H2O(l) + 4e− → 4OH−(aq)
Fe2+ is then further oxidised by O2 to hydrated iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3·xH2O = rust.

Methods of corrosion prevention

MethodHow it works
PaintingA physical barrier that seals the metal surface so water and oxygen cannot reach it. Fails once the paint is scratched or chips off.
Oil / greaseA barrier layer (used on moving parts) that repels water and excludes oxygen; must be reapplied as it wears or washes away.
GalvanisingCoating the iron/steel with zinc. This is a barrier and sacrificial protection — zinc has a more negative E° (more easily oxidised), so even if the coat is scratched the zinc corrodes preferentially and protects the iron.
Cathodic protectionForcing the iron to be the cathode so it cannot oxidise. Done either with a sacrificial anode — a more reactive metal (Mg or Zn) connected to the iron is oxidised instead — or with an impressed current from an external DC supply that feeds electrons to the iron.
Electrolysis

10Electrolysis

What is a non-spontaneous redox reaction? A redox reaction that will not proceed on its own because it is energetically unfavourable (E°cell is negative). It requires a continuous input of electrical energy from an external DC power supply to be driven forward. Electrolysis therefore converts electrical → chemical energy — the opposite of a galvanic cell.
What stays the same as a galvanic cell — and what flips Oxidation is always at the anode and reduction always at the cathode; electrons always travel anode → cathode. The polarity flips: in electrolysis the anode is positive (+) and the cathode is negative (−).

Electrolysis of a molten salt (inert electrodes)

Only the ions of the salt are present, so they are discharged directly. For molten sodium chloride:

Anodic (+): 2Cl− → Cl2(g) + 2e−
Cathodic (−): Na+ + e− → Na(l)

Electrolysis of an aqueous solution (inert electrodes)

In water, H2O itself can react, competing with the dissolved ions, so there are two possibilities at each electrode:

Two possible anodic (oxidation) reactions
the anion is oxidised — e.g. 2Cl− → Cl2(g) + 2e−
or water is oxidised — 2H2O → O2(g) + 4H+ + 4e−  (E° = +1.23 V)
Two possible cathodic (reduction) reactions
the cation is reduced — e.g. Mn+ + n e− → M
or water is reduced — 2H2O + 2e− → H2(g) + 2OH−  (E° = −0.83 V)
How the most likely reaction at each electrode is chosen
  • Cathode: the species most easily reduced — the one with the highest (most positive) E° — reacts.
  • Anode: the species most easily oxidised — the one with the lowest (most negative) E° reduction potential — reacts.
  • Then allow for practical factors: concentration (a concentrated ion may discharge in preference to water, e.g. concentrated Cl− → Cl2) and overpotential (extra voltage needed to release gases, especially O2).

11Applications of Electrolysis

Electroplating (e.g. silver plating)

  • Anode: the pure plating metal — Ag(s) → Ag+(aq) + e−.
  • Cathode: the object to be plated — Ag+(aq) + e− → Ag(s).
  • Electrolyte: a solution containing ions of the plating metal (e.g. silver nitrate, AgNO3).
  • Applications: decorative jewellery and cutlery, corrosion protection, improving appearance or surface conductivity.

Electrowinning (e.g. extraction of aluminium from alumina, Al2O3)

  • Anode: 2O2− → O2(g) + 4e−  (the carbon anodes then burn away: C + O2 → CO2).
  • Cathode: Al3+ + 3e− → Al(l).
  • Electrolyte: Al2O3 dissolved in molten cryolite (Na3AlF6) at ~950 °C, which lowers the melting point (Hall–Héroult process).

Electrorefining (e.g. purification of impure "blister" copper)

  • Anode: the impure copper — Cu(s) → Cu2+(aq) + 2e− (it dissolves).
  • Cathode: a thin sheet of pure copper — Cu2+(aq) + 2e− → Cu(s) (pure copper deposits).
  • Electrolyte: acidified copper(II) sulfate solution (CuSO4 / H2SO4).
  • Anode slime: less-reactive impurities (Ag, Au, Pt) that do not dissolve fall below the anode as valuable "anode sludge"; more reactive impurities (e.g. Fe, Zn) stay dissolved in solution.

Recharging secondary cells (e.g. the lead accumulator)

An external supply drives the discharge reactions in reverse (this is electrolysis); the electrode that was the cathode on discharge becomes the anode during charging.

  • Anode (oxidation) on charge: PbSO4(s) + 2H2O(l) → PbO2(s) + 4H+(aq) + SO42−(aq) + 2e−.
  • Cathode (reduction) on charge: PbSO4(s) + 2e− → Pb(s) + SO42−(aq).

Quantitative electrolysis (Faraday's laws)

Method (1) Q = I × t  (t in seconds) → (2) n(e−) = Q ÷ F, F = 96 500 C mol−1 → (3) use the half-equation ratio to get mol of product → (4) convert to mass (m = nM) or gas volume.
Worked example — silver plating A current of 2.00 A flows for 30.0 min through AgNO3. Mass of Ag deposited?
Q = It = 2.00 × (30.0 × 60) = 3600 C
n(e−) = 3600 ÷ 96 500 = 0.03731 mol
Ag+ + e− → Ag (1 : 1) ⟹ n(Ag) = 0.03731 mol
m = 0.03731 × 107.9 = 4.03 g Ag
