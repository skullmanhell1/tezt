Answers

Multiple choice

Q1 — (A). Calculate the TOT each year:

Year 1: 100/100 × 100 = 100.0
Year 2: 110/106 × 100 = 103.8   ← rose
Year 3: 115/110 × 100 = 104.5   ← rose again

The TOT rose in both periods, so it improved from Year 1 through to Year 3. Trap: in Year 3 the XPI rose 5 points and the MPI rose 4 points — both rose, so you must actually compute the ratio rather than eyeball it.

Q2 — (D) Risen / Decreased. This question deliberately gives you volumes and prices, and you need different columns for each half.

TOT (prices only):  Y1 = 100/100 × 100 = 100
                    Y2 = 110/105 × 100 = 104.8   → RISEN
Net export VOLUME:  Y1 = 100 − 100 = 0
                    Y2 = 105 − 110 = −5          → DECREASED

Export volume rose 5% but import volume rose 10%, so net exports fell even though the terms of trade improved. This is the single most instructive question in the set — it separates the price effect (TOT) from the volume effect (net exports). Don't use price data for a volume question or vice versa.

Q3 — (d) the terms of trade have improved.

Year 1: 100/100 × 100 = 100.0
Year 2: 110/120 × 100 =  91.7
Year 3: 120/125 × 100 =  96.0

The trap is (c). Year 3 (96.0) is still below Year 1 (100), so it's tempting to say "deteriorated". But the question asks about the change in Year 3, i.e. from Year 2 — and 91.7 → 96.0 is a rise. (a) and (b) are the classic "you cannot infer values or volumes from price indexes" distractors: with no quantity data you can say nothing about the current account or export volumes.

Q4 — (b) A widening of the current account deficit. A fall in the TOT means export prices fall relative to import prices, so export credits fall relative to import debits → BOGS falls → the CAD widens. Why the others fail: (a) a falling TOT causes a depreciation, not an appreciation, as demand for AUD falls; (c) a falling TOT reduces growth prospects; (d) it reduces national economic welfare by cutting real purchasing power from trade.

Q5 — (d) decrease national income and decrease the trade balance. Same mechanism: lower export prices relative to import prices → lower export revenue → trade balance falls; that lost revenue is lost income to exporters → national income falls. Both go the same way, which rules out (b) and (c) immediately.

Q6 — (d) The current account deficit is $143 billion.

Net goods    = 340 − 316 = +24
Net services =  58 −  61 =  −3
BOGS         =  24 −   3 = +21
CAB          =  21 + (−164) = −143   → deficit of $143 billion

Why the others fail: (a) BOGS is $21b, not $27b; (b) $340b is exports of goods, not the goods balance (which is $24b); (c) $164b is the net income deficit, not the CAD.

Q7 — (d) the capital account must record a deficit. From the identity CAB + KAFA = 0, a current account surplus must be exactly offset by a capital and financial account deficit. Why the others fail: (b) is backwards — a CAS means net foreign liabilities stop growing, not grow rapidly; (c) describes what happens under a deficit.

Be aware: option (a) — "reduced need for foreign investment" — is also economically true, since a CAS means savings exceed investment. The reason (d) is the keyed answer is the word "must": (d) follows necessarily from the accounting identity, whereas (a) is an economic implication. Note also that the question says "capital account" loosely to mean the whole capital and financial account.

Q8 — (c) financial account. Direct investment is a financial account component. It's a credit because Australia's liabilities to the rest of the world increase. The trap is (b) — the capital account is the small, separate part that records capital transfers and non-produced non-financial assets (brand names, copyrights, mining rights). Investment in a business goes in the financial account.

Q9 — (c) B has a comparative advantage in producing iron ore. Compare opportunity costs:

Country A:  1 coal costs 100/50 = 2 iron ore  |  1 iron ore costs 50/100 = 0.5 coal
Country B:  1 coal costs 320/80 = 4 iron ore  |  1 iron ore costs 80/320 = 0.25 coal

Lower opportunity cost wins. Coal: A (2 iron ore) beats B (4) → A has the comparative advantage in coal. Iron ore: B (0.25 coal) beats A (0.5) → B has it in iron ore. So (a) and (d) are wrong. (b) is wrong because B produces more of both goods, so B has the absolute advantage in both.

Check your method on a row where the answer is given: 2016 → (83,588 − 89,868) + (−9,327) = −6,280 − 9,327 = −15,607 ✓ matches the table. The method is sound.

(b) Two structural reasons for the widening net income deficit, 2016 → 2017 (5 marks)

The net income balance moved from −$9,327m to −$13,486m, a widening of the deficit by $4,159m. Note the question asks for structural reasons — fundamental factors affecting the income balance, not cyclical ones.

1. The savings–investment gap. Australia has a small population and a relatively low national savings rate, creating a gap between intended investment and available domestic savings. Australia closes this gap by borrowing overseas (foreign debt) and selling ownership of Australian assets (foreign equity). Both generate servicing obligations — interest on debt and dividends and profits on equity — recorded as primary income debits. As the gap persisted through this period, continued capital inflow added to these outflows, widening the net income deficit.

2. The growing stock of accumulated foreign liabilities. Because Australia has run persistent current account deficits funded by capital inflow, the stock of foreign debt and foreign equity has accumulated over time. A larger stock mechanically requires larger interest and dividend payments each year. In addition, much of Australia's capital stock — particularly mining, which is largely foreign-owned — became more profitable in 2017 as commodity prices recovered (visible in the 16.5% jump in G&S credits), so higher profits meant higher dividends paid to overseas investors, further widening the primary income deficit.

(c) The relationship between the annual growth rate and BOGS (5 marks)

There is an inverse relationship. When Australia's growth rate rises, national income rises, so households increase consumption spending — particularly on discretionary and luxury goods — increasing consumption imports. Firms simultaneously increase production and output, increasing imports of capital and intermediate goods. Both raise debits in the balance on goods and services, reducing BOGS. When growth slows, income falls, households cut discretionary spending and firms reduce capital imports, so debits fall and BOGS improves.

The data support this. The highest growth rate in the period, 2.8% in 2015, coincided with the largest BOGS deficit (−$11,305m), driven by G&S debits peaking at $94,022m. Conversely the lowest growth rate, 1.8% in 2017, coincided with BOGS moving into a surplus of $7,882m, with debits falling to $89,494m.

Qualification worth a mark: the 2017 improvement was not caused by weaker growth alone. G&S credits rose sharply from $83,588m to $97,376m (16.5%), reflecting a recovery in commodity prices and a rising terms of trade. So while the inverse growth–BOGS relationship holds, export prices were the larger driver in 2017.

A note on Q9 and scope

Q9 is comparative advantage, which is not one of the five dot points in your task statement. It appeared in the past-exam set your teacher included, which mixes questions from across the trade topic. I've worked it because it's cheap insurance, but don't spend study time there at the expense of the five listed points.

The same applies to the exchange rate. It appears inside your topic as a factor affecting BOGS and the TOT — which is covered — but exchange rate determination itself is a separate syllabus point and isn't listed in your task.
